Trump's Eldest Son Profits From His Father's Second Term
Trump Jr. is a magnet for MAGA capital. His ventures are faltering.
By Aidan Williams
05/05/25 • 11 minute read

President Donald Trump is more than a patriarch of the Trump family: He is the face of a potent brand with a massive following in politics and business.

While past presidents divested their financial interests during their terms, Trump embraces his presidential clout to enrich himself and his family.

During the first year of his second term, the Trump family's overall net worth has increased by more than $1.4 billion, much of it through promoting, selling and licensing products marketed to Trump's populist MAGA movement.

While much of the Trump family has taken advantage of Trump’s blend of politics and commerce, one of the biggest beneficiaries has been the eldest son, Donald Trump Jr.

After decades working in real estate and hospitality as an executive at the family-held Trump Organization, the president's eldest son turned his attention toward a new and more low-profile avenue to make money: venture capital.

His dealings drew scrutiny from Sen. Elizabeth Warren last week after Unusual Machines, a drone maker with which he is involved, was awarded $70 million in Defense Department contracts amid weapons procurement for the Iran conflict. The Massachusetts Democrat called the deals “corruption in plain sight” and questioned whether the Pentagon, led by Trump appointee Pete Hegseth, is “just a cash machine for Trump’s children.”

Typically, public companies, or those preparing to go public, bring Trump Jr. on as a board member, paying him millions in cash and stock options for his services. In exchange, ethics experts say companies look to tap into Trump Jr.’s “extensive political network” to open doors in Washington and fast-track the company for growth.

“The Trump family has made it quite clear that the easiest way to influence government policy is to help them make money,” said Donald Sherman, the executive director of Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington, a government watchdog group that advocates for accountability and transparency in Washington.

An examination of the longer-term stock performance of three firms fronted by Trump Jr. in the past year reveals that each of them has lost since he assumed a position with the firms, as his political connections have failed to reverse the fortunes of struggling companies.

Unusual Machines

Three weeks after Trump’s 2024 electoral victory, Unusual Machines, a little-known drone maker, was propelled to fame after announcing that the president’s son had joined its advisory board. An S-1 filing revealed that Trump Jr., who owned 131,580 shares acquired through a private placement offering, received an additional 200,000 shares as part of his advisory agreement.

“Don Jr. joining our board of advisors provides us the unique expertise we need as we bring drone component manufacturing back to America," said CEO Allan Evans in a press release. In the filing, the drone maker, which is “heavily dependent on Chinese exports,” warned that then-President-elect Trump’s threatened tariffs could “materially and adversely affect” its business.

“The need for drones is obvious," said Don Jr. in the release, "I love what Unusual Machines is doing to bring drone manufacturing jobs back to the USA and am excited to take on a bigger role in the movement.”

Despite the company’s concerns, shares of Unusual Machines rose more than 250% to a high of $23.62 in the week following Trump Jr.’s appointment.

“All of these investors are looking for themes, and a lot of these themes are being turbocharged by Trump and the inner circle,” said Matthew Tuttle, CEO of Tuttle Capital Management, which monitors online investor forums where Trump Jr.’s name draws attention. Tuttle owns shares of two ventures backed by the president’s son, Unusual Machines and GrabAGun.

You’ve got a double whammy with Unusual Machines. You’ve got the drone theme, and you’ve got Trump Jr.’s involvement.”

Yet over a year into Trump's second term, the perceived benefits of Trump Jr.'s involvement failed to materialize for Unusual Machines. According to its Q1 2025 earnings report, Trump’s China tariffs forced the company to place “larger inventory orders during the tariff pause” to avoid “reduced margins and higher costs."

While shares of Unusual Machines nearly tripled from their April low amid efforts by the U.S. military to grow its drone stockpile, the stock is still down nearly 30% from its Trump Jr.-fueled highs.

GrabAGun

In June, GrabAGun, an online firearms retailer in which Trump Jr. is a board member, went public in a SPAC merger with Colombier Acquisition Corp. II, run by Republican party megadonor Omeed Malik.

SPACS, or Special Purpose Acquisition Corporations, are blank-check companies that raise funds through private and public investment to merge with a company and take it public.

Malik also serves as president of the investment firm 1789 Capital, where Trump Jr. is a partner.

“Today is an exciting moment and major milestone for our company,” said GrabAGun CEO Marc Nemati in a press release, “I look forward to taking advantage of the many opportunities in front of GrabAGun to enhance our platform and expand our market presence."

Shares of the SPAC surged roughly 70% to an all-time high of $18.45 in the lead-up to the merger as investors raced to take a stake in the firm.

Around this time, GrabAGun executives “got to go to Saudi Arabia and talk with the sheiks” during President Trump’s state visit, according to Tuttle. Business executives in attendance included Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Amazon CEO Andy Jassy.

Trump Jr. promoted the company’s debut in 10 posts to his over 15.2 million followers on X, alongside multiple TV appearances.

In the hours after Trump Jr. rang the New York Stock Exchange opening bell to cheers of “USA” from the trading floor, shares of GrabAGun sank by nearly 20% to $13.12 per share.

As of the market close on Tuesday, May 5, the stock was trading at $2.90 per share, less than a third of its pre-merger high.

“I don’t think guns is ever going to be a real hot theme like drones is,” said Tuttle, “If we assume that, at least compared to Biden, Trump is a more law-and-order president, you could make an argument that maybe a couple of years ago people were thinking of buying guns, but now, maybe not.”

PublicSquare

PublicSquare, an e-commerce site that describes itself as catering to “patriotic Americans” looking to “shop their values and put purpose behind their purchases,” went public in 2023 through a separate SPAC merger with Colombier Acquisition Corp.

“There’s really this cool parallel economy ecosystem that really starts with consumer spending. We hate ESG and we hate DEI,” said PublicSquare CEO Michael Seifert in an interview with Breitbart, “We have big hopes for the future. We’re going to take over commerce and bring the power structures of society back toward we the people.”

In December, Trump Jr. joined the company’s board of directors, sending shares surging over 250% to a midday high of $7.70.

“PublicSquare has a distinct position in the market based on the core tenets of our nation’s founding,” said Trump Jr. in a press release, “The American people have affirmed the importance of liberty, and PublicSquare is at the forefront of this movement.”

Despite investor optimism, Trump Jr.’s appointment did little to solve the company’s difficulties turning a profit.

The stock is down nearly 95% from its Trump Jr.-fueled high, with shares hovering near $0.80. Trading volume has declined from a high of 173 million to less than half a million as of Tuesday, May 5.

“People on the right don’t invest based on their values, while people on the left are much more willing to do that,” said Tuttle.

Checks and Balances

Federal corruption laws bar government employees and their direct family members from engaging in business ventures that could involve a conflict of interest, shielding investors from volatility driven by political favoritism. However, presidents, vice presidents and their families are largely exempt from such rules, complicating the path toward accountability.

The only strict statutes prohibiting these families from engaging in corruption are the Constitution’s emoluments clauses, which bar the president and vice president from receiving gifts or bribes from foreign governments, Congress or individual states. However, the rule does not apply to gifts from state-backed companies, private entities or any dealings involving the president’s family.

“Anti-corruption for presidents has traditionally been built on precedent, not law,” said Danielle Brian, president of the Project on Government Oversight, a nonpartisan government watchdog group. “It seems quite clear that they [Trump’s family members] aren’t viewing any traditional checks and balances as restraints.”

While Congressional Democrats have introduced bills to codify longstanding anti-corruption norms for the executive branch into federal law, a lack of bipartisan support has left those measures stalled in the House.

“Congress has capitulated and the Supreme Court has largely ruled in their favor,” said Sherman. “What rules would they think would apply to them?”

That lack of enforceable constraints helps explain why investors continue to back Trump Jr.-linked companies, even as their performance falters.

“Having the president's ear has its advantages,” said Tuttle, “I wouldn’t be surprised to see Trump come to his son’s rescue at some point.”

Others, including Melanie Sloan, a former federal prosecutor and government ethics expert, argue that Trump’s limited tenure and “lame duck” status could ultimately weaken the political influence underpinning those bets.

“People are only investing in these companies because of Donald Trump,” said Sloan, a long-time advocate for stronger ethics rules in Washington. “You really have to wonder what will happen to any of these the second Donald Trump is no longer in office.”
